I have not read FPLGuru's code since June.

I do not mean that I skim it. I mean I do not open it. Before Fable 5, I used to skim what the model wrote, and sometimes I looked at the structure or the architecture. Since Fable 5, and now with Opus 5.5, I do not think I have seen any code.

In September, 3,858 commits landed in FPLGuru that were not merges. 156 of them carry my name, and 154 of those carry a `Co-Authored-By: Claude Fable 5.1` line, because an agent wrote them in a session I ran. The rest came from agents working for DK and me.

If you are an engineer, that first paragraph probably makes you nervous. It made me nervous too. This post is about why I am comfortable with it now: who writes the code, the rules they work under, the gates that check them, the months when it went badly, and what is left for the two humans.

## The constitution

Most "I let AI write my code" posts skip this part. I think it is the only part that matters.

Agents in FPLGuru work under a set of documents in the repo. The order they arrived in is a timeline of the workflow growing up:

| Document | Added | Added by |
|---|---|---|
| `CLAUDE.md` | 21 Dec 2025 | Me |
| `AGENTS.md` | 6 Mar 2026 | Me |
| `CONTRIBUTING.md` | 19 Apr 2026 | DK's agent session |
| `docs/ADVISOR_MODEL.md` | 1 Jul 2026 | Codex Automation |

The first instructions were written by a human for the machines. The most detailed process document, the one about how models should *think*, was written by a machine.

The core idea of that document fits in one line from it: advisors decide and de-risk, builders implement.

- **Advisor models** handle ambiguity. They turn a request into a spec, a plan and GitHub issues with acceptance criteria.
- **Builder models** take one issue at a time. They branch, write a failing test first, implement only the agreed scope, check it, and open a pull request. If a builder wants to change the product direction, it must send the question back to an advisor instead of deciding.

And one rule sits above the rest, in the project conventions: **"Never auto-commit, always ask before committing."** Agents can read anything, write anything, run anything and propose anything. Inside a session, they ask before they make it permanent.

`CLAUDE.md` is no longer a small note. It is the file you have to read before you touch anything, and it is long because every hard lesson in this post ended up in it.

## Fable 5: the week the agents surprised me

Every builder I know who works with agents has a moment when the tool stopped being a tool. Mine was Fable 5.

Its first co-author line in our repo is dated 9 June 2026. I would give it a small feature task, and it would work out all the changes that the feature needed and the blast radius of each one. It made the changes to match. Then it wrote tests on its own, when the prompt only said to implement the feature. Nobody asked it for tests. It just knew a careful engineer would write them.

As I remember it, Fable 5 was pulled from release for about a week by the US government, and then came back. In that first week, I think it was probably the best model in the world. I had the feeling we had entered a whole new era of AI.

You can see it in the log. In June, "dk" made 363 commits and Claude made 110. Then on 25 June, a new author appeared: Codex Automation. By August it was the largest author in the repo for the month, with 697 commits. The humans had stopped being the people who type.

## Opus 5 and the slop

Then we made a decision for money, and it nearly stopped the project.

Fable 5 was much better than anything before it, but it was too expensive to run all day. So we moved the work to Opus 5. Its first co-author line is dated 25 July.

Opus 5 was the worst model I have worked with. It would hallucinate. It would skip instructions. It wrote super technical jargon and long, twisted English sentences. I came very close to pausing all development.

DK's answer was not to give up on the agents. It was to build a better harness for them. He built a "software factory" skill called **Olympus**, which arrived in the repo on 28 August. Olympus carefully steers several agents through a complex task. Each agent gets one role and its own charter, inside a fixed workflow. With Olympus, a model we did not trust alone could still do good work as part of a team.

I want to be fair to Opus 5, because the log is fair to it. On 2 August, an agent with an Opus 5 co-author line wrote this into a commit message:

> 3\. GREEDY ROUTING WAS A REGRESSION, and it was my change.

Then it gave the measurement that proved it wrong, and reverted itself. I have worked with humans who would not write that sentence. The lesson I took is that the value of the system is not that the machines never make mistakes. It is that the system makes their mistakes show up as evidence.

## The reviewer that never ran out of comments

Every pull request in FPLGuru gets an automated code review. DK built the reviewer, and for a stretch of the summer it ran on GPT-5.6 Sol.

Sol was a difficult reviewer. It always found something to fix, and it gave its review one comment at a time. Fix that comment, and the next round found another. That time was hard, but we powered through.

On 19 August, one of DK's agents wrote a retrospective with the title "five PRs, seventy-three review rounds." It studied five of my pull requests. Two cleared in five rounds each. The other three took 13, 25 and 25. The in-app feedback form alone took 25 rounds and about 59 findings.

The surprising conclusion: every finding the reviewer raised was correct. The reviewer was not the problem. The loop was. A fix made in a hurry carried its own new bug, and the next round found it. The number of rounds had nothing to do with how many real problems the first version had. It only measured how patient the reviewer was.

We did not slow the agents down. We wrote rules that tell them when to stop. They are in `CONTRIBUTING.md` today, and a builder reads them before it touches code:

- **Answer a finding with a failing test first.** The first commit reproduces the problem in red. The fix lands on top.
- **Two rounds in a row that find bugs in your own fixes is a repair stop.** The next comment is not another fix. You name the part of the system, say what the fixes keep getting wrong, revert that part, and check its design again.
- **A comment cannot authorise more patching.** That sentence is there because it is exactly what an agent will ask for.

And the rules are under test. A test file pins the key sentences word for word. If anyone, human or machine, softens the repair-stop rule, a test fails.

Two days later, on 21 August, we switched on a **spec-review lane**. Before a large change is built, an automated reviewer reads its spec, especially the "Assumptions" section: every "X is not needed, because Y" that the design depends on. And on 26 August, the label `codex:good-to-merge` first appeared. When the code reviewer is satisfied, it adds that label, and an automation merges the pull request.

Since 1 September, 2,094 merge commits have landed on `develop`. Eleven of them have my name on them.

## Trusting the gates

So how can I ship code I do not read? Because I do not trust any single agent. I trust the gates around them.

A change in FPLGuru passes through:

1. **A spec**, for anything large, reviewed by the spec-review lane before any code is written.
2. **A builder**, writing the test first, on its own branch.
3. **Local CI.** Since 25 September, a pull request into `develop` does not run cloud CI. The agent runs the full suite on its own machine and pushes a proof commit, and the merge gate checks that proof.
4. **The code reviewer**, with the repair-stop rules above.
5. **Feature flags and config switches.** Most new features ship behind a switch that we can turn off without a deploy, and the risky ones ship switched off. If something is wrong in production, we flip a value in a table.

None of those gates is clever on its own. Together, they mean I do not have to be the last line of defence, because I am not the only line.

## Where it still goes wrong

I promised this series would be honest, so here is the record of things that went wrong, from the log and not from memory.

**The bug the agents found but did not cause.** On 5 August, an agent pushed `fix(security): stop sending Supabase JWT in API request URLs and bodies`. The frontend had been putting the user's session token into a URL, where proxies log it and browser history keeps it. I checked where that line came from, because I knew the easy story. It came from a commit of mine in April 2025, sixteen months before an agent found it.

**The feature built and reverted on the same day.** On 4 August, an agent built a Stripe checkout and webhook service. On the same day, it was reverted, and a decision record says billing is deferred. The agent did good work. We decided the product was not ready for it. Building is cheap now. Deciding is still expensive, and deciding is the one thing the agents do not do for us.

**Before all of this, there was the Cursor night.** I told this story in the first post: one request to add a refresh button broke my whole HTML, CSS and JavaScript codebase, and I paid $20 for Windsurf just to fix it. The difference between then and now is not that the models stopped making mistakes. It is that every mistake now hits a test, a reviewer, a stop rule or a switch before it hits a user.

## Opus 5.5 and round the clock

The first Opus 5.5 co-author line is dated 23 September. It already has more co-author lines than any other model in the repo: 2,910.

If Fable 5 started a new era, Opus 5.5 put us in another orbit. It hits the sweet spot that Fable 5 could not hit for us: it is good enough to trust with large tasks, and cheap enough to run all day. That is why September looks the way it does. Claude alone made 2,332 non-merge commits that month.

The way DK and I work now is simple to describe.

We sync every day, on a call or on Slack. We decide what needs to be built. We discuss the high-level design or concept. We point out the problems and the flaws in each other's thinking. When we agree, we hand it to our agents.

I am in India and DK is in California. Our time zones used to be a problem. Now they are a feature. One of us hands work over at the end of the day, and agents keep working while that person sleeps. We are rarely both awake, but FPLGuru ships around the clock.

Today even the project management is partly an agent. I write a request in a project chat. A coordinator agent opens a thread for it, a thread agent does the work and opens a pull request, the reviewer reviews it, and the automation merges it. I read the results. I will tell that story properly in the last post of this series, about Matchday.

## What is left for the humans

So what do DK and I actually do?

The whole game now is to think. We push back on a lot of design changes that the agents propose. The agents push back on a lot of our review comments, and they are often right. And we keep pushing the agents to take on more complex tasks than we think they can handle, because they keep proving us wrong.

We set the goals. We decide what not to build. We decide when to turn a feature on. We choose which model to pay for and which harness to build around it. We write the rules for when to stop. None of that is coding in the sense I learned the word. All of it is the job.

## What I would tell you

If you are an engineer or an engineering manager, here is what this year taught me.

**Write the rules down, in the repo, where the agents read them.** Your conventions are not real until they are in a file an agent loads before every task. Ours started as a small note and became the most important file we have.

**Do not trust a model. Trust a system of gates.** One reviewer, one test suite or one model will fail. A stack of cheap, independent checks fails much less often.

**Teach your agents when to stop.** Agents never get tired, so they will patch forever. The most useful rule we wrote was not about how to build. It was about when to revert.

**The model matters more than you want it to.** Opus 5 nearly ended this project. Fable 5 and Opus 5.5 changed what two people can do. Try every new model on your real work, quickly, and be ready to change your harness when the model changes.

**Spend your time on thinking.** The typing is not the job any more. The deciding is.

Two years ago I was one person with a GPT-4 prompt that did not know Sokratis had left Arsenal. Today I am one of two people directing a team of agents that ships thousands of commits a month. I still do not read the code. I read the decisions.
