There's no shortage of Engineering Managers (EMs) writing about the job these days. What's missing is who actually trains us for it.

I barely get time to hit flow state anymore. Our team has a no-meeting rule on Fridays, and one Friday, with nothing on the calendar, I finally got back into code I'd been putting off for days. It made me wonder how other managers ever find that flow state, and honestly, it made me understand why so many leads want to go back to being ICs.

But you can't run a team of only ICs, not unless you're under 50 people. The dysfunction would go through the roof. Most engineers already have underdeveloped communication skills, and if you strip out interaction with the team, the manager, the stakeholders, they drift even further from anything resembling a cohesive, high-functioning team.

So then, who trains the managers? We were the same ICs, with the same underdeveloped communication skills. I went through first-time manager training when I got promoted, and it was good. I learned to run 1:1s. I learned to give performance feedback. What it didn't teach me was the messy middle: the moments where the textbook answer doesn't exist and you just have to decide.

I've had three of those moments stay with me.

The first was a scheduling nightmare that had nothing to do with scheduling. My Android lead's grandfather was in the hospital. My only other staff engineer was already buried under two critical customer issues. Then a third came in. There was no one left to throw at it, so I rolled up my sleeves myself, triaged it down to the actual root cause, and found a workaround that unblocked the customer without putting any more load on the one developer I had free. Nobody trains you for "the bench is this thin, go fix it yourself."

The second wasn't a fire to put out, it was a slow leak. We'd acquired a team, and one of the leads who joined us was a pure people manager, no hands-on technical work at all. Post-acquisition, that wasn't going to fly, every lead was expected to be technical. He said yes every time I raised it, then quietly did nothing, always busy with 1:1s or Jira or standups. I pushed, gently, then less gently, until we sat down for a real conversation with a real plan and a real deadline attached to it. He agreed again, and again nothing changed. Eventually the team stopped waiting on him and started routing technical calls straight to me. That's when I knew it was over. I opened a formal PIP. He rejected it outright, said he never wanted to be a tech lead in the first place, and we parted ways.

The third one wasn't even my decision to make. My iOS lead had to put one of his junior developers on a PIP, and he was dragging his feet, visibly uncomfortable with it. I told him straight: that developer's performance was going to reflect on him, whether he acted or not. The call was his to make, but I'd walk him through the HR process myself. That one taught me something different, that some of the hardest days as a manager aren't about the decisions you make, they're about the ones you have to hand to someone else and trust them to make.

None of this showed up in a slide deck. I learned all three the hard way, alone, in the moment, because there was nobody ahead of me to ask. And now I watch the same thing happening to the managers coming up behind me. They hit these exact walls, and I want them to ask. Mostly, they don't.

Maybe that's just the job. We're the last of the EMs, and until someone builds the training that actually covers this, I'll keep figuring it out in public, and keep the torch alive as long as I can.
